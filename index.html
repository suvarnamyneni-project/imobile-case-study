Redesigning a mobile banking experience

02 — Context

India’s first mobile banking app

ICICI Bank is described as India’s largest private bank, with a presence in 16 countries. In 2008 it launched iMobile, and the case study calls that launch India’s first mobile banking application.

By the time of this project, iMobile served over 10 million users. The people the redesign had to consider were wider than the app’s own user base: the bank’s customers, across age groups, totaling over 100 million. Those two counts are different populations, and the write-up keeps them separate.

03 — The challenge

Simple, reachable, and still complete

ICICI Bank aimed to revamp its iMobile app for iOS, focusing on delivering an intuitive and delightful experience. Our task was to design a comprehensive personal banking app that seamlessly integrates diverse functionalities while catering to a broad user base with varying financial needs. The challenge was to create a user-centric interface that balances simplicity, accessibility, and efficiency, ensuring the app remains both functional and engaging for a diverse audience.

The tension in that brief is the whole project. A comprehensive app pulls toward more features, more products, and more chances to introduce something new. A diverse audience pulls toward fewer steps, plainer language, and a home screen that does not assume everyone banks the same way.

04 — What we knew going in

Best in India, and still under the global line

Forrester’s analysis, as the case study recounts it, found that Indian banking apps performed poorly against international ones. No Indian app surpassed the global average of 65 out of 100. ICICI received the highest rating among the Indian apps, at 63 out of 100. The report’s name and year are not given.

Even with those shortcomings, ICICI remained the most popular app and was seen as one of the best among its competitors. Stakeholder interviews added a view of how the app worked, where it was limited, and which issues were already known. Some restrictions set by the Reserve Bank of India could not be changed, so they sat outside the design space from the start.

05 — Process

Discover, then design, then test again

The work ran in three passes. Discover and explore came first, then design, then test and evaluate. Client feedback and another round of refinement sat inside the last pass, before the interface was treated as final.

07 — User interviews and personas

Fifteen interviews, three portraits

The target users spanned all age groups, the same 100 million customers. To understand how often people came to the app, where it hurt, and whether the most-used features could be found, Suvarna interviewed 15 people: eight ICICI customers and seven customers of other banks. Those conversations, plus data from the client, became three personas.

The interview guide, other quotes, and any affinity map are not in the write-up. What follows is what the three persona cards themselves say. Preference scores are the filled segments on each card’s five-part bars.

08 — Competitive landscape

Feature-rich, and easy to get lost in

No Indian bank app stood out as ideal in design, features, and usability. That included the direct competitors, HDFC and SBI. The comparison then widened to indirect competitors: Dhani, Walnut, and Paytm. There is no scoring matrix in the write-up.

ICICI’s own app was already notable for how much it held, including UPI payments, loan applications, and card design. The gap was discoverability. Important functions were not easy to find, so the work turned toward making them easier to discover and easier to reach.

The slideshow has six screens: SBI, HDFC, Paytm, Google Pay, and Walnut Prime twice. Dhani is named in the text and has no screen. Google Pay has a screen and is not named in the text. The two Walnut Prime files are the same image.

09 — Heuristic review

Balance checking never made the home screen

A heuristic evaluation of the old dashboard found usability problems: taps that were not needed, navigation that wound around, and forms that ran long. The processes were more complex than the task. The recommendations were to reduce friction, make things clearer, and simplify the workflows.

The annotated findings sit beside the screen below. The screenshot itself is not marked up. The list is the review, in the case study’s words.

  1. The layout was stagnant.
  2. Notifications and account updates were not visible on the dashboard.
  3. “My Favourite Transaction,” which people did not use often, was given a prominence it did not need.
  4. The grid showed many services, not ordered by what that person actually did.
  5. The profile area took 30 to 35 percent of the screen.
  6. Balance checking, the most-used action, was not on the dashboard at all.
  7. A hamburger menu hid the actions.
  8. There was no place to discover a new feature, and no personalized suggestion.

10 — Design objectives

Four things the new app had to do

The objectives are short, and they answer the review directly. The home screen had to offer the frequent actions. Features had to be findable. Content had to belong to the person looking at it. And the phone’s own capabilities were fair game if they made the experience better.

  1. 1Quick access to frequently performed actions.
  2. 2Better feature discoverability.
  3. 3Display user-relevant content.
  4. 4Leverage available technology to enhance the experience.

11 — Information architecture

Frequent work first, the rest still close

The information architecture was created and then refined: unnecessary states came out, pages moved, and three areas came in — Expenditure Analysis, Upcoming Transactions, and Discover. Frequently used services sit first. Less common features stay quick to find.

Adding and managing products was streamlined: lines of credit, insurance, debit cards, and investments. The map also looks for moments to engage someone without interrupting them, a conversational prompt that does not break the task they came to do.

12 — Wireframes

The short path and the business path

Several concepts were drawn for the pages that carry the product: Login, Dashboard, Accounts, Expenditure Analysis, Fund Transfer, Bill Pay, Offers, and Help. Some of those concepts made the journey simpler and, in doing that, worked against the business goals of feature discoverability and cross-selling financial products.

The final choices had to hold both. A screen that only serves the task hides the rest of the bank. A screen that only promotes products makes the task longer. The wireframes are where that trade was made, page by page.

13 — Dashboard exploration

A home screen with three jobs

The dashboard is the gateway to everything else. It had to show the accounts a person holds, open a way into more features and products, and still put the actions they already repeat within reach.

Balance checking was the most-used feature, so it belongs up front. Not everyone wants that number large on an open screen. The wireframes add one step for that reason: “tap to view balance.” Search stays prominent, with voice input, because of how many features ICICI has. Help stays within reach. Top Activities is filled from what that person does most often. A further band of analytics, suggestions, and tips is there so the bank feels as if it has been paying attention.

Research noted in the wireframes suggests most people hold a single product, usually a savings account. The layout leans on that primary relationship instead of asking someone to pick through every product variant first. Across the frames below, some savings cards show a Check Balance control and others show the amount already revealed.

14 — Solutions

Five changes on the path people already take

The same complaint shows up more than once in the solution notes: transaction narration was vague and inconsistent, so people could not tell what a line in their history actually was. Clearer narration is the through-line under the spending views and the history itself.

Personalized from the get-go

The homepage is personalized from activity. Top Activities highlights tasks the person already does. Discover introduces banking tools and services they have not used. On the Discover side of these frames, that introduction is an expense feed, an upcoming-transaction feed, and tips.

Budgeting and comparisons

Monthly expenses can be tracked visually and compared. Spending patterns are drawn out, and a person can set a budget as a monthly spending limit. The spending-overview screen puts income and spends on one bar, then offers an estimated budget drawn from the past six months.

Expenditure analysis

Expenses break down by category, and the person is alerted when a category budget is crossed. This is also where the narration problem was named: if the description of a transaction is vague, the category is a guess. The suggestion was a clearer narration pattern, so the breakdown could be trusted.

Upcoming transactions

Spending patterns were used to spot what recurs — an EMI, a bill, rent — and to say so before the date. The same narration problem was raised again here: a warning is only useful if the person can tell what it is for. On the screen, each item carries an amount and a due date.

Transaction history

History is meant to be readable. Transactions are tagged automatically, and the person can change a tag or add one. Filter and sort work in more than one way. This piece is tied directly to the feedback about narration: if the bank’s description is unclear, the tag is the person’s way to name it.

15 — Designed details

The decisions inside the wireframes

The annotated board goes further than the eight key pages. These are the decisions written on it. They are design rationale from the wireframes, not a claim about what later shipped.

Before login

Notifications can be reached without logging in. A returning user can see the balance with a swipe, still without logging in. Actions that do not need a session have a place. Fingerprint and Face ID are the login.

Balance, search, and help

Account balance is up front, behind “tap to view balance,” for privacy. Search is visible, typed or spoken. Help stays within reach. The layout favors the primary savings relationship over a menu of every product.

Discover

Five parts: an upcoming-transaction feed predicted from the credit cycle and usage, service requests such as a new ATM card or a cheque book, a spending overview, offers, and tips. Tips are also a place to cross-sell, a credit card for example, without a separate detour.

Money in and money out

Upcoming transactions come from the last few months of payments. Spending overview is the month’s credits and debits, with earlier months, a custom period, and a swipe into categories. Service requests show what is open or recently closed, with a status strip and a tap for the detail.

Accounts

New and returning customers see different landings, with a help icon and persuasive messages in place of a static block. Several savings accounts are a swipe apart. Account details can be shared, and the person chooses which details. Transactions filter by type, mode, time, amount, cheque, and history up to three years. A bill or a transfer can start from the account. Transactions can be bookmarked, the category edited, and the communication address updated from home. Dates are clearly separated.

Fixed deposits

The deposit is drawn so the money can be seen growing, meant to reassure and to lower withdrawal. FD advice sits with the account, and applying for another deposit includes an explanation of the benefits.

Bills and feedback

Upcoming bills are visible together. Categories follow how often that person pays them. Auto Pay is offered, and a biller can be added, edited, or removed. Feedback is a single tap. Ratings use emoji rather than a scale from 1 to 10, and leftover questions can be answered now or later.

Services, offers, and cards

The services list highlights what is used most and can be searched. Offers that are already approved for that person sit on the first fold, in clear categories. Some are contextual to place and time, with distance shown, and the category can change without scrolling back to the top. A voucher’s amount and quantity are set in one gesture, and a coupon code copies on tap. For an Expressions card, the person picks a category and a design, sees it large, and swipes to the next design.

18 — Outcome

Primary tasks in the open, the rest still findable

The end product was what we envisioned it to be. A beautiful and functional banking app that was one-of-its-kind in India. The app allows the user to perform all primary tasks seamlessly. It also makes those not-so-commonly-used features discoverable if the user ever needs them. To ensure that the app remained relevant in the years to come, we integrated some simple yet trending features like payments via Siri (voice-enabled) and QR codes; login via Touch ID, Face ID, and voice recognition; and Siri-enabled in-app voice search.

That is the outcome as written: primary tasks are seamless, and the uncommon features can still be found. The features named for staying relevant are Siri payments, QR-code payments, login with Touch ID, Face ID, and voice recognition, and in-app search through Siri.

19 — What I learned

Don’t get married to an idea

Working on this project has taught me a lot about the design thinking process, the do’s and don’ts of mobile design (especially when it comes to iOS), and the importance of not getting married to an idea.

The role note sets that lesson in a first major UX role, inside a fast-paced agency environment. The learning curve was steep. Once the speed and the scope were familiar, the work could settle on a design that was user-centered and, in the words of that note, user-friendly.

The wireframe trade-off is the same lesson in practice. A journey that was ideal for the user still had to make room for discoverability and for the products the bank needed people to find. Holding both, rather than defending the first concept, is the decision the case study describes.
