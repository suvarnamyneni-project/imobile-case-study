Case Study · ICICI Bank

Redesigning a mobile banking experience

ICICI Bank set out to revamp iMobile for iOS: a personal banking app with a wide set of tasks, built for people whose financial lives do not look alike. The work was to keep it simple, reachable, and still complete.

03

Problem

A comprehensive app pulls toward more features. A diverse audience pulls toward fewer steps, plainer language, and a home screen that does not assume everyone banks the same way.

ICICI Bank aimed to revamp its iMobile app for iOS, focusing on delivering an intuitive and delightful experience. Our task was to design a comprehensive personal banking app that seamlessly integrates diverse functionalities while catering to a broad user base with varying financial needs. The challenge was to create a user-centric interface that balances simplicity, accessibility, and efficiency, ensuring the app remains both functional and engaging for a diverse audience.

ICICI Bank is described as India’s largest private bank, with a presence in 16 countries. In 2008 it launched iMobile, and the case study calls that launch India’s first mobile banking application. By the time of this project, iMobile served over 10 million users. The people the redesign had to consider were wider than the app’s own user base: the bank’s customers, across age groups, totaling over 100 million. Those two counts are different populations, and the write-up keeps them separate.

Forrester’s analysis, as the case study recounts it, found that Indian banking apps performed poorly against international ones. No Indian app surpassed the global average of 65 out of 100. ICICI received the highest rating among the Indian apps, at 63 out of 100. The report’s name and year are not given. Even with those shortcomings, ICICI remained the most popular app and was seen as one of the best among its competitors.

Some restrictions set by the Reserve Bank of India could not be changed, so they sat outside the design space from the start.

Balance never made the home screen

Balance checking was the most-used action in the desk research. On the old dashboard it was not there at all.

The screen spent itself on the wrong things

The profile area took 30 to 35 percent of the screen. “My Favourite Transaction,” which people did not use often, was given a prominence it did not need. A hamburger menu hid the actions.

Hard to find, hard to name

The app already held a lot, including UPI, loans, and card design. Important functions were not easy to find. Transaction narration was vague and inconsistent, so a line in history often did not say what it was.

Heuristic review, in the case study’s words

A heuristic evaluation found taps that were not needed, navigation that wound around, and forms that ran long. The processes were more complex than the task. The recommendations were to reduce friction, make things clearer, and simplify the workflows.

  1. The layout was stagnant.
  2. Notifications and account updates were not visible on the dashboard.
  3. “My Favourite Transaction,” which people did not use often, was given a prominence it did not need.
  4. The grid showed many services, not ordered by what that person actually did.
  5. The profile area took 30 to 35 percent of the screen.
  6. Balance checking, the most-used action, was not on the dashboard at all.
  7. A hamburger menu hid the actions.
  8. There was no place to discover a new feature, and no personalized suggestion.

End Users

The target users spanned all age groups. The redesign had to serve the app’s users and, beyond them, the bank’s customers. The write-up keeps those two populations separate.

iMobile users

Over 10 million people already on the app. Desk research ranked what they open it for, longest bar first: balance checking, account statement, recent transactions, offers and promotions, then bill payment. The bars are relative. The chart does not print a count for each bar.

ICICI Bank customers

Over 100 million customers, across age groups. This is the wider group the redesign had to consider, not a second count of app users. Their financial lives do not look alike: several accounts and family bills, a first salary and a trip to save for, or a retirement that should be quiet.

Customers of other banks

Seven of the 15 interviews were with customers of other banks, alongside eight ICICI customers. The conversations asked how often people came to an app, where it hurt, and whether the most-used features could be found.

Mobile already carried ordinary banking. People used it to look at a balance, scan recent transactions, pay a bill, or move money. The same complaint shows up more than once later in the work: if the description of a transaction is vague, the person cannot tell what the line actually was.

User Personas

Representative personas from the case study. Fifteen interviews, plus data from the client, became these three portraits. Preference scores are the filled segments on each card’s five-part bars.

SA

Shivnay Agarwal, 39, data scientist

Responsible, practical, disciplined, cautious. Married, with two kids, several accounts, investments, credit-card bills, and family expenses. He wants to know where every rupee goes.

“I like to stay up to date, be it new technology or bill payments.”

Goals: instantly access all account details, and keep track of bills and pay them on time. Pain points: hard to search for a transaction, no easy overview of all accounts, and irrelevant content. Bars out of five: speed 3, features 4, help 3, discover 3.

AM

Athira Menon, 25, fashion designer

Carefree, no dependants, tech-savvy, confident. She recently moved cities, her parents live abroad, and she is saving for a trip to France while spending on dining out.

“I want more control over my expenses and want to save more to spend on travel.”

Goals: track expenses, save more to travel, quickly view funds, and do day-to-day transfers. Pain points: cannot quickly view her balance, no way to track expenses, and the app is not aesthetically pleasing. Bars out of five: speed 5, features 3, help 1, discover 3. Help is the last thing she is asking for.

HS

Hiten Shah, 56, bank manager

Content, not tech-savvy, jolly, weary. Retiring after 33 years. He wants one place for his banking, with time left for gardening, cricket, and his granddaughter.

“I’ve seen enough and more of life. Looking for some peace and quite after retirement.” The card uses the wording “peace and quite.”

Goals: retire financially secure and lead a hassle-free life. Pain points: help is not easy to find, he is not aware of investment opportunities, and the app is not intuitive. Bars out of five: speed 2, features 2, help 5, discover 4.

Design Decisions

The objectives answer the review directly. The annotated wireframes then hold two jobs at once: a short path for the task, and a way for the bank’s other products to stay findable. These notes are design rationale from the wireframes, not a claim about what later shipped.

Quick access

Quick access to frequently performed actions. Balance checking belongs up front. Top Activities is filled from what that person does most often.

Discoverability

Better feature discoverability. Less common features stay quick to find. Discover introduces banking tools and services the person has not used.

Relevant content

Display user-relevant content. The layout leans on the primary savings relationship. Research noted in the wireframes suggests most people hold a single product, usually a savings account.

Use the phone

Leverage available technology to enhance the experience. Search accepts voice input. Login can be fingerprint or Face ID. The outcome later names Siri payments, QR codes, Touch ID, Face ID, voice recognition, and Siri search.

Some concepts made the journey simpler and, in doing that, worked against the business goals of feature discoverability and cross-selling financial products. A screen that only serves the task hides the rest of the bank. A screen that only promotes products makes the task longer. The wireframes are where that trade was made, page by page.

Before login

Notifications can be reached without logging in. A returning user can see the balance with a swipe, still without logging in. Actions that do not need a session have a place. Fingerprint and Face ID are the login.

Balance, search, and help

Account balance is up front, behind “tap to view balance,” for privacy. Not everyone wants that number large on an open screen. Search is visible, typed or spoken, because of how many features ICICI has. Help stays within reach. The layout favors the primary savings relationship over a menu of every product.

Discover

Five parts: an upcoming-transaction feed predicted from the credit cycle and usage, service requests such as a new ATM card or a cheque book, a spending overview, offers, and tips. Tips are also a place to cross-sell, a credit card for example, without a separate detour. On the home screen, Discover sits beside Top Activities.

Clearer narration

Transaction narration was vague and inconsistent, so people could not tell what a line in their history actually was. Clearer narration is the through-line under the spending views, the upcoming list, and the history itself. If the bank’s description is unclear, the tag is the person’s way to name it.

Money in and money out

Upcoming transactions come from the last few months of payments. Spending overview is the month’s credits and debits, with earlier months, a custom period, and a swipe into categories. Service requests show what is open or recently closed, with a status strip and a tap for the detail.

Accounts

New and returning customers see different landings, with a help icon and persuasive messages in place of a static block. Several savings accounts are a swipe apart. Account details can be shared, and the person chooses which details. Transactions filter by type, mode, time, amount, cheque, and history up to three years. A bill or a transfer can start from the account. Transactions can be bookmarked, the category edited, and the communication address updated from home. Dates are clearly separated.

Fixed deposits

The deposit is drawn so the money can be seen growing, meant to reassure and to lower withdrawal. FD advice sits with the account, and applying for another deposit includes an explanation of the benefits.

Bills and feedback

Upcoming bills are visible together. Categories follow how often that person pays them. Auto Pay is offered, and a biller can be added, edited, or removed. Feedback is a single tap. Ratings use emoji rather than a scale from 1 to 10, and leftover questions can be answered now or later.

Services, offers, and cards

The services list highlights what is used most and can be searched. Offers that are already approved for that person sit on the first fold, in clear categories. Some are contextual to place and time, with distance shown, and the category can change without scrolling back to the top. A voucher’s amount and quantity are set in one gesture, and a coupon code copies on tap. For an Expressions card, the person picks a category and a design, sees it large, and swipes to the next design.

A home screen with three jobs

The dashboard is the gateway. It had to show the accounts a person holds, open a way into more features and products, and still put the actions they already repeat within reach. A further band of analytics, suggestions, and tips is there so the bank feels as if it has been paying attention.

Prototype

Information architecture, annotated wireframes, eight dashboard directions, and five changes on the path people already take. The write-up does not include a public prototype URL.

Information architecture

The information architecture was created and then refined: unnecessary states came out, pages moved, and three areas came in — Expenditure Analysis, Upcoming Transactions, and Discover. Frequently used services sit first. Less common features stay quick to find. Adding and managing products was streamlined: lines of credit, insurance, debit cards, and investments. The map also looks for moments to engage someone without interrupting them, a conversational prompt that does not break the task they came to do.

Five changes on the path people already take

Personalized from the get-go

The homepage is personalized from activity. Top Activities highlights tasks the person already does. Discover introduces banking tools and services they have not used. On the Discover side of these frames, that introduction is an expense feed, an upcoming-transaction feed, and tips.

Budgeting and comparisons

Monthly expenses can be tracked visually and compared. Spending patterns are drawn out, and a person can set a budget as a monthly spending limit. The spending-overview screen puts income and spends on one bar, then offers an estimated budget drawn from the past six months.

Expenditure analysis

Expenses break down by category, and the person is alerted when a category budget is crossed. This is also where the narration problem was named: if the description of a transaction is vague, the category is a guess. The suggestion was a clearer narration pattern, so the breakdown could be trusted.

Upcoming transactions

Spending patterns were used to spot what recurs — an EMI, a bill, rent — and to say so before the date. The same narration problem was raised again here: a warning is only useful if the person can tell what it is for. On the screen, each item carries an amount and a due date.

Transaction history

History is meant to be readable. Transactions are tagged automatically, and the person can change a tag or add one. Filter and sort work in more than one way. This piece is tied directly to the feedback about narration: if the bank’s description is unclear, the tag is the person’s way to name it.

Test, evaluate, repeat

Testing was phase by phase. Wireframes were refined with feedback from the internal team, and with feedback from the client, before the interface was designed.

The user sessions were quick and in the moment: 8 to 10 internal people, from various departments. The case study does not list the tasks, the findings one by one, or any success rate.

Outcome

Primary tasks in the open, and the uncommon features still findable. The write-up does not add a rating, an adoption figure, a task time, or a date, and it does not say whether this version shipped.

The end product was what we envisioned it to be. A beautiful and functional banking app that was one-of-its-kind in India. The app allows the user to perform all primary tasks seamlessly. It also makes those not-so-commonly-used features discoverable if the user ever needs them. To ensure that the app remained relevant in the years to come, we integrated some simple yet trending features like payments via Siri (voice-enabled) and QR codes; login via Touch ID, Face ID, and voice recognition; and Siri-enabled in-app voice search.

What the app had to do

Primary tasks are seamless: the balance, the statement, a transfer, a bill. Features that are not used every day can still be found, through search, Discover, and a services list that calls out what that person uses most.

What was added to stay relevant

Payments via Siri, QR-code payments, login with Touch ID, Face ID, and voice recognition, and in-app search through Siri. These are the features the outcome names.

What is left unstated

No project year, no agency name, and no launch result. Sample dates on the screens, including December 2017 and January 2018, are not treated as the project date. The open question the write-up leaves is whether, or when, this version shipped.

What I learned

Working on this project has taught me a lot about the design thinking process, the do’s and don’ts of mobile design (especially when it comes to iOS), and the importance of not getting married to an idea.

The role note sets that lesson in a first major UX role, inside a fast-paced agency environment. The learning curve was steep. Once the speed and the scope were familiar, the work could settle on a design that was user-centered and, in the words of that note, user-friendly.

The wireframe trade-off is the same lesson in practice. A journey that was ideal for the user still had to make room for discoverability and for the products the bank needed people to find. Holding both, rather than defending the first concept, is the decision the case study describes.
